## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This memo identifies **20 priority transit stations** for Transit-Oriented Communities (TOC) zoning reform in Philadelphia, based on recent multifamily development activity (2020-2025) within 1/4 mile of rapid transit.

**Key Findings:**

- **14,600 multifamily housing units** permitted within 1/4 mile of high-frequency transit since 2020
- **Top 20 stations split into two categories:**
  - **8 Expansion Stations:** Already have 500-foot TOD overlays → expand to 1/4 mile
  - **12 New Stations:** No existing TOD → add new 1/4-mile zones
- **21 stations (32%)** sit on council district boundaries, requiring joint decisions
- **Political strategy:** Expansion stations provide easier "on-ramp" before adding new stations

## IV. POLICY FRAMEWORK & POLITICAL STRATEGY

### **Core Argument: Housing Production IS the Community Benefit**

The market has validated TOC-eligible areas with **14,600 units** built near transit without TOC policy. This ordinance channels existing demand toward transit, climate, and equity goals.

**Community Benefits Delivered:**

1. **Housing Production:** 14,600 households with transit access = reduced car dependency, lower transportation costs
2. **Transit Ridership:** More riders = better service, more frequent trains, improved mobility for existing residents
3. **Economic Development:** Vibrant commercial corridors around transit nodes = local business growth, job creation
4. **Climate & Equity:** Reduced sprawl, lower VMT, concentrated investment in historically underserved areas

### **Inclusionary Zoning (IZ) Position**

- Not opposed in principle, but **only if fully funded** (Baltimore model with development subsidies)
- **Caution:** Unfunded IZ mandates risk reducing production (see MIN zones analysis)
- **Preference:** Allow market-rate TOC to proceed; use property tax increases to fund affordable housing trust

### **Community Benefits Agreements (CBAs): Not Recommended**

- CBAs create project-by-project barriers that slow housing production
- TOC should be **rule-based and predictable**, not discretionary
- Community benefits are embedded in policy itself

## V. IMPLEMENTATION PATHWAYS

### **PLAN A: Citywide Ordinance (STRONGLY RECOMMENDED)**

**Scope:** All 20 priority stations (8 expansion + 12 new)
**Radius:** 1/4 mile per mayor's proposal

**Advantages:**
- **Simpler:** Single ordinance vote vs. 21 inter-district boundary negotiations
- **Equitable:** Same rules citywide; no neighborhoods left behind
- **Faster:** One decision vs. multi-year district-by-district process
- **Stronger:** 21 boundary stations (32% of total) require joint approvals anyway

**Path:** Coordinate with Mayor's Office; build coalition with pro-housing council members (Gauthier D3, Squilla D1, etc.)

---

### **PLAN B: Two-Phase District Approach (FALLBACK)**

If citywide ordinance fails, pursue two-phase strategy:

**Phase 1: Expansion Stations**
- 8 MFL stations with existing 500ft TOD → expand to 1/4 mile
- Affects Districts 1, 3, 4, 7 only
- Lower political lift (precedent exists)

**Phase 2: New Stations + Full Coverage**
- Add 12 new stations (Districts 1, 2, 5, 7, 8, 9)
- Include Regional Rail for Districts 4, 6, 8, 9
- Build on Phase 1 success

**Risk:** Prolonged timeline, uneven coverage, complex boundary negotiations

---

### **PLAN C: State-Level Reform (LONG-TERM)**

If local reform fails entirely:
- Pending state legislation mandates **1/2-mile TOC zones** around all SEPTA stations
- **Impact:** 17,000 units vs. 14,600 at 1/4 mile (+16%)
- **Advantage:** Bypasses hostile local councils, uniform statewide standards
- **Timeline:** Multi-year advocacy campaign

- Regional Rail stations could become future nodes if service frequency improves

### **Boundary Stations: Use as Citywide Ordinance Argument**

**Message:** "21 boundary stations requiring inter-district coordination = strong case for simple citywide rules. Why negotiate 21 separate agreements when one ordinance treats everyone equally?"
