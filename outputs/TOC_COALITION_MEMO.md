## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This memo presents a data-driven approach to Transit-Oriented Communities (TOC) zoning reform in Philadelphia, analyzing recent multifamily development patterns (2020-2025) within 1/4 mile of transit stations to identify priority areas for upzoning.

**Key Findings:**

- **14,600 multifamily housing units** have been permitted within 1/4 mile of high-frequency transit (BSL, MFL, trolleys, Regional Rail) since 2020, demonstrating strong existing market demand
- **Top 10 stations** account for 5,279 units (36% of total activity), showing concentrated development pressure
- **Critical geographic gap:** Districts 2, 4, 6, 8, and 9 have minimal subway coverage but contain viable Regional Rail stations that should be included
- **Market-based approach:** Recent permit data shows development is already happening near transit—TOC would legalize and expand what the market is demanding

---

## I. CITYWIDE APPROACH: THREE SCENARIOS

Analysis at three radius scenarios reveals trade-offs between political feasibility and housing production:

### **Option A: Expand Existing TOD Overlays (500 feet → 1/4 mile)**

- **Current 500-foot radius:** ~1,300 units (49 permits)
- **Proposed 1/4-mile radius:** ~5,000 units (291 permits)
- **Impact:** +283% increase in captured development
- **Political advantage:** 13 MFL stations already have 500-foot TOD zones; framed as "expanding proven policy"

### **Option B: Mayor's Proposal (1/4 mile, recommended)**

- **BSL/MFL (30 stations):** 4,800 units
- **+ Trolleys (506 stops):** +8,400 units → **13,200 total** (+175% increase)
- **+ Regional Rail (36 stations):** +1,400 units → **14,600 total** (+11% increase)
- **Recommendation:** Include BSL, MFL, trolleys, and select Regional Rail stations
- **Rationale:** Balances significant housing production with service frequency standards (6-20 min subways/trolleys vs hourly+ Regional Rail)

### **Option C: State Bill Standard (1/2 mile)**

- **Total impact:** ~17,000 units (990 permits)
- **Impact:** +1,205% vs. current 500-foot overlays
- **Political path:** If local ordinance fails, this remains long-term goal for state-level reform

**RECOMMENDED APPROACH:** Option B (1/4 mile radius for BSL/MFL/Trolleys, plus select high-ridership Regional Rail stations)

## IV. POLICY FRAMEWORK & POLITICAL MESSAGING

### **Core Argument: Housing Production IS the Community Benefit**

The market has already validated TOC-eligible areas: **14,600 units** have been built near transit without TOC policy. This ordinance does not create development pressure—it **channels existing demand** toward equitable, climate-friendly, economically productive locations.

**Community Benefits Framework:**

1. **Housing Production:** 14,600 units produced = 14,600 households with transit access, reduced car dependency, and lower transportation costs
2. **Transit Ridership:** More riders = better service, more frequent trains/buses, improved mobility for existing residents
3. **Economic Development:** Transit nodes become vibrant commercial corridors, supporting local businesses and job creation
4. **Climate & Equity:** Reduced sprawl, lower VMT (vehicle miles traveled), concentrated investment in historically underserved transit corridors

### **Inclusionary Zoning (IZ) Position**

- **Not opposed in principle**, but only if **fully funded** (e.g., Baltimore model with development subsidies)
- **Caution:** Unfunded IZ mandates risk reducing production (see MIN zones analysis showing IZ requirements correlating with development suppression)
- **Preference:** Allow market-rate TOC development to proceed, use property tax revenue increases to fund dedicated affordable housing trust fund

### **Community Benefits Agreements (CBAs): Not Recommended**

- CBAs create project-by-project negotiation barriers that slow housing production
- TOC policy should be **rule-based and predictable**, not subject to discretionary approvals
- Community benefits are embedded in the policy itself (housing, transit, economic development)

## V. POLITICAL STRATEGY RECOMMENDATIONS

### **Plan A: Citywide Ordinance (Preferred)**

- **Scope:** All BSL, MFL, and trolley stops + select high-ridership Regional Rail stations
- **Radius:** 1/4 mile (consistent with mayor's proposal)
- **Advantage:** Uniform rules, no neighborhood left behind, maximizes housing production
- **Path:** Requires majority City Council support; coordinate with Mayor's Office

### **Plan B: District-by-District Opt-In (Fallback)**

If citywide ordinance fails:
- Ask each Council Member to designate **3-5 priority stations** in their district
- Use rankings above to guide recommendations
- **Risk:** Geographic inequity, some districts may opt out entirely
- **Mitigation:** Continue state-level advocacy for mandatory TOC (HB/SB pending)

### **Plan C: State-Level Reform (Long-Term)**

- Pending state legislation would mandate **1/2-mile TOC zones** around all SEPTA stations
- If local reform fails, pivot to state advocacy as ultimate solution
- **Impact:** 17,000 units vs. 14,600 at 1/4 mile (+16% increase)

## VI. ADDRESSING OPPOSITION

### **Districts 5 & 7: "Anti-Development" Council Members Preside Over Highest Activity**

**Challenge:** Council Members in Districts 5 and 7 are characterized as "anti-development," yet these districts contain 9 of the top 10 citywide stations and account for **4,821 units** (33% of total).

**Response:**
- Emphasize that development is **already happening** in these districts—TOC simply ensures it happens near transit
- Frame as **protecting neighborhood character**: Better to have density on transit corridors than scattered throughout residential side streets
- **Data point:** Both Girard stations (District 5) = 1,632 units already built. TOC would formalize what the market has validated.

### **Districts 2, 6, 9: Low Activity Areas**

**Challenge:** Some districts have minimal development activity near transit, raising questions about whether TOC would have any impact.

**Response:**
- TOC creates **option value** even in low-activity areas—future market shifts could unlock development
- **Equity argument:** Low-income neighborhoods deserve same upzoning opportunities as high-income areas
- Regional Rail stations in these districts could become future development nodes if service frequency improves

## APPENDICES

**A. Methodology:**
- Permit data: OpenDataPhilly, 2020-2025, R-2 occupancy type (official multifamily >2 dwellings)
- Clustering: 1/4 mile (402 meters) radius from station geocodes
- Scoring: 100-point system weighing recent development (50%), market momentum (25%), DVRPC existing orientation (15%), DVRPC future potential (10%)
- District assignment: Spatial join using City Council district boundaries

**B. Data Sources:**
- Philadelphia Department of Licenses & Inspections (L&I) building permits
- DVRPC Transit-Oriented Development Station Database (2018)
- SEPTA station locations and service frequencies
- City Council district boundaries (OpenDataPhilly)
